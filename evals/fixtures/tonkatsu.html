The Tonkatsu That Saved My Twenties

By Amelia Hartwell — 74 comments

It was a rainy Tuesday in Osaka when I first understood what a pork cutlet could be. I was twenty-three, freshly heartbroken, and surviving on convenience-store onigiri and misplaced optimism. My landlady, the formidable Mrs. Tanaka, took one look at me dripping in her genkan and decided — the way only landladies of a certain age can decide — that I needed feeding.

Her kitchen was two burners and a fish grill, and yet from it came the crispiest, most golden thing I had ever eaten. I have spent the eleven years since chasing that cutlet through six apartments, two countries, and one deeply regrettable air fryer phase. My husband says I talk about Mrs. Tanaka more than my own mother. He is not wrong.

Every family has a dish that marks time, and this is ours now. My daughter asks for it on birthdays. I make it when the weather turns, because in my heart tonkatsu season begins with the first cold rain, the way it did that Tuesday in Osaka.

A few notes before we begin, because the comments will ask: yes, you must use panko, and no, ordinary breadcrumbs will not do; I will simply never forgive you. Mrs. Tanaka double-breaded nothing and neither do we.

Ingredients (serves 4)

  • 4 boneless pork loin chops, about 2 cm thick
  • 1 tsp fine salt
  • ½ tsp white pepper
  • 60 g plain flour
  • 2 large eggs, beaten
  • 120 g panko breadcrumbs
  • Neutral oil for shallow frying (about 2 cm depth)
  • ¼ head white cabbage, shredded as finely as you can manage
  • Tonkatsu sauce, to serve

Method

  1. Snip the fat rim of each chop in a few places so it fries flat, then pound gently to an even 1.5 cm. Season both sides with the salt and white pepper.
  2. Set up three shallow dishes: flour, beaten egg, panko. Coat each chop in flour, shake off the excess, dip through the egg, then press firmly into the panko so it clings in a thick, even coat.
  3. Heat the oil in a heavy pan to 170°C. Fry the cutlets two at a time, 4–5 minutes per side, until deep golden and cooked through.
  4. Rest on a rack — never paper towels, which steam the crust — for five minutes. Slice into strips against the grain.
  5. Serve over the shredded cabbage with tonkatsu sauce, and rice if the day has been long.

If you make this, tell me in the comments whether it rained. It always tastes better when it rained.
